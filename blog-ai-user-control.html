AI Is Helpful, but Knowing When Not to Use It Matters

AI has become part of how I work and study, but researching how other students use it has made me think more about its limitations and the importance of user control.

I use AI pretty regularly. Whether I'm troubleshooting code, researching something new, or working through ideas, I've found it can be incredibly useful.

But I also don't think AI is automatically the best solution for everything.

As someone studying Human-Centered Design and Development, I'm interested in more than what AI can do. I want to understand how people actually interact with it, what makes them trust it, and when they decide they'd rather do something themselves.

What Students Actually Do With AI

In one of my research projects at Penn State, my team studied how five undergraduate students used AI while completing academic work.

What I found interesting was that their comfort with AI wasn't necessarily consistent across different tasks.

Students could find it useful for getting explanations or working through information, but more visual or complex tasks sometimes led them to question the responses and return to their own resources.

I think that's an important distinction.

It's easy to discuss AI adoption in terms of whether someone uses it or not, but that doesn't really capture how people make decisions while using it.

And because this was a small exploratory study, our findings aren't meant to represent every college student. They did, however, give us useful questions to explore.

Designing AI Around People

The research made me think more about how AI tools could better support people's decision-making.

For example, what if an AI interface made it easier to verify information, recognize uncertainty, or switch back to doing something manually?

Those might seem like relatively small features, but they could make a difference in how comfortable someone feels using the technology.

These questions connect to broader conversations about responsible AI. The National Institute of Standards and Technology's AI Risk Management Framework addresses factors such as reliability, transparency, and trustworthiness in AI systems.

What I'm Taking Forward

I've also had opportunities to build with AI myself, including creating a gallery question-and-answer application using Python, LangChain, and the OpenAI API.

Working on both the development and research sides has given me a better appreciation for the difference between making a technology function and making it useful to people.

I still find AI exciting, and I think there's a lot of potential in how we apply it.

But the more I work with it, the more I believe that knowing when to use AI is just as important as knowing how.
