Just Because It Looks Good Doesn't Mean It's Good UX

Apple's Liquid Glass design raises an interesting question about the relationship between creativity and usability. How do we know when a design is doing too much?

I'll admit, I'm a big fan of visually interesting design. I love experimenting with layouts, color, typography, and different ways to make an interface feel unique.

But I've also learned that being visually impressive and being easy to use aren't necessarily the same thing.

One of the most interesting examples of this is Apple's Liquid Glass design, introduced with iOS 26.

The update brought translucent elements, fluid animations, and a more layered appearance to Apple's interface. It's definitely a creative direction, but it has also sparked discussions about whether some of those visual effects make everyday tasks more difficult.

And I think that's a conversation worth having.

When Design Starts Competing With Usability

One of the biggest challenges with a highly visual interface is maintaining clarity.

For example, transparent buttons and menus might look modern, but if they blend into the content behind them, users may have a harder time recognizing where to click.

Movement is another consideration. Animations can make an interface feel responsive and engaging, but if everything is constantly shifting, those effects can become distracting.

In an analysis of Liquid Glass, Nielsen Norman Group raised concerns about legibility, navigation consistency, and some of the new interaction patterns.

I find this interesting because Apple is known for its attention to design, yet even a company with extensive resources faces difficult decisions about balancing innovation and usability.

Creativity Still Matters

I don't think the answer is to make every interface completely minimal or remove anything that isn't strictly functional.

That would be pretty boring, honestly.

Visual design helps establish personality, communicate information, and make experiences more enjoyable. A well-designed animation can provide useful feedback, and an interesting layout can make a product feel memorable.

The important part is understanding why those choices are being made.

Does the animation help someone understand what happened? Does the new navigation make information easier to access? Or is a design element mainly there because it looks cool?

I think those questions should be part of the design process, especially when introducing something new.

Finding the Balance

Something I've come to appreciate about UX is that there isn't always one perfect solution.

Designers have to consider people's expectations, accessibility, visual appeal, and the overall experience. Sometimes those priorities compete, and finding the right balance takes testing and iteration.

I think Apple deserves credit for continuing to experiment with its visual identity. At the same time, new design trends shouldn't be adopted just because they're new.

For me, the most interesting design work happens when creativity and usability support each other.

After all, what's the point of a beautiful interface if people struggle to use it?
